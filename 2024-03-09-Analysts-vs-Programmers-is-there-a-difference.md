# Analysts vs. Programmers, is there a difference?

This article is a primer for those wishing to understand programmers and analysts a little better. It is full of generalisations that are not supported by anything other than experience and personal anecdotes. It is not supposed to be a definitive definition of either role. Merely a guide as to how best spot the talent available and to then get the most out of it.

For reasons that will not be explored in this piece there is a prevalence of the idea that all “geeks/nerds” are cut from the same cloth. This is a sweeping statement based on anecdotal evidence. The thing is it is often accepted as a general truth. The proof of this is in the fact that as soon as I mention I develop computer software I am frequently presented with the inanest and at times ridiculous requests. This is not to be dismissive of these requests for assistance. Frequently I end up getting involved in things I did not set out to get involved with.

This is not dissimilar to other professions in the world. Plumbers and electricians will often be a little guarded at acknowledging their respective trades lest they be called in to do a “quick job”. Frequently accompanied by the phrase "should only take a minute”. If you should find yourself offering assistance you can be absolutely assured to be the first port of call should anything, and I really do mean anything, should happen to anything (I really do mean anything) connected to a device “you fixed”. “Fixing” of course relates to anything you do, which includes turning it off and on again.

This is how the world sees programmers and analysts. They just see people who are “geeks/nerds” who do things that seem odd. Like Morris dancing or re-enactment societies. They laugh at math jokes and talk about topics with a seemingly impenetrable language.

The fact is that this view is very wrong in several ways. But rather than deconstruct the fallacies of others, let me create a fallacy or two of my own.

The skillset required of an analyst and a programmer relies on very different facets.

* An analyst is an explorer. Someone who likes to know things that others do not. A person who can see patterns in information. They can scrutinise the past or foretell the future. Historians or prophets.
* A programmer is a builder. Someone who can abstract real world problems into a language that can be understood by machines. They solve problems in an automated and repeatable fashion.

This is not to say that there are not a vast amount of transferable skills between the two disciplines, as there is so much in common. But ultimately, they are very different disciplines that require different core attributes.

So far, this piece has covered the ethereal and anecdotal, ignoring some key facts. There is and has always been a shortage of both analysts and programmers. As Moore’s Law has predicted some aspects of technological growth, other laws should be considered in relation to the supply and demand of these rarer resources. Other disciplines also vie for the attention of people with skills associated with analytics and programming.

Probably the most glaring indicators of demand is renumeration and skill requirements. These parameters offer an insight into the landscape that is analysts and programmer demand. One of the interesting things to note is the range of wages and qualifications required. This is mentioned only because it is a source of proof of the scarcity of people who are analysts and/or programmers. Do not set the expectation of easily being able to find the talent you are looking for.

In addition to this is the fact that not all analysts or programmers are equal in ability. In fact, I think it would be safe to say that there are some in these fields that are there simply in it for the money. This is not to say that they are not capable individuals. Just potentially not capable at analysis or programming. They can however derail a project in short order while deflecting the blame elsewhere. This point can be driven home by accepting that a “hello world” program is not the same as a Single Page Application (SPA) using Server-Side Rendering (SSR). The thing is, sometimes all that is needed is a “hello world” program.

Part of this inequality in ability is how the world has reacted to a shortage of competent analysts and programmers. Frequently a large project will be designed by a technical authority. They lay out the framework for how the solution is to be resolved. Once the architectural work is completed many of the programmatical tasks fall into set patterns of repeatable tasks. There is no need for a deep understanding of programming, system design or a host of other activities associated with a good analyst or programmer. Legacy systems are also a sinkhole for resources with similar issues. All the problems that attract good analysts and programmers are likely already considered solved in the current solution.

The range of analytical and programming tasks is immense in size and complexity. Take a minute to think of all those antiquated legacy systems that are “critical infrastructure” that must be maintained. These essential activities are not driving the fields of analytics or programming forward. They exist solely to preserve existing models of profitability. These systems are increasing in number and complexity. These are not new systems to address new problems.

As a cautionary note, there is the rarest of rare in a person who is both a very capable analyst and programmer. Terms such as “rockstar” or “unicorn” are bandied about the place when speaking of such people. They do exist. But their scarcity is very real. But the potential that they have to reap great rewards is immense. Any doubters need only look to some of the largest companies in the world. Google and Microsoft for instance exist solely because of programmers and analysts.

So how best to spot such individuals, you may ask? This is easy, at least in principle. But as is so often the case, the practical elements are a smidgen more problematic. Talking to a candidate is by far the best way to determine their interests. Ultimately you need confidence that you can work with them and that they can deliver the results you need. But they also need confidence that you will provide the tools, support, and space to deliver the desired results.

There have been times when I have been scolded for my “fizz buzz” solution as not being “perfect”. In almost the same breath the hiring organisation will admit to not having a formal testing department, no contingency, no understanding let alone mapping of technical debt while offering a fraction of the market rate. Safe to say these were not very long interviews.

Contrary to this is the approach used by larger organisations. Frequently they will focus on a cultural fit as opposed to exact skill matching. This is likely due to their ability to offer in-house training to ensure that the correct level of skills is present. Quite simply, the larger institutions have the funding to enable them to develop talent.
