Knowing Things

Now I would forgive you for thinking that knowing things is pretty easy. You just know some stuff right? Let me see if I can change your mind on this, if you should hold such thoughts.

If I were to ask you “what is the population of London?”, how would you answer it? Give an approximation based on some number you think you may have heard of at one point? Will you turn to a source of knowledge such as a government website or Wikipedia? Perhaps you will even go “When?”, as you appreciate the difficulties in giving an answer.

In trying to answer this question we generally accept that any answer offered is wrong, but the degree of error is acceptable. At least in the instances where we consider an answer correct. The population of London is in a state of flux. People come and go all the time. Are all the people even tracked and what do we mean with the definitions of London or population? It is soon apparent that there is considerable scope for variance even for all answers considered to be correct.

Perhaps we should try something a little easier. Describe the Sun as it is now (NOTE: Do not directly observe the Sun, use a pinhole camera or similar.). What colour is it and can you spot any features or patterns? Technically you would have to wait about 8 minutes or so and then look. You see, you have never seen the Sun as it is “now”. You have always observed the Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago.

But despite the age of the light we see from the Sun (the data as it were), we are still able to use it and understand it. At least to a degree, as we would be foolish to say we know everything about our Sun.

With these two simple examples we can see imperfect information still being perfectly usable and offering utility. We also get a hint at some of the costs that would be involved in improving the quality of the information. Of course, we also get to see that there seems to be a constraint on the speed at which information can travel through the Universe.

Information about things is a system that does work for us. Even with the causal delays and difficulty in detailed description. By mechanisms that we do not understand at this time we are able to parse vast quantities of data. Not only that, but we can also move about in it and manipulate it. Even more amazingly we can make predictions about the future based on it. The most amazing aspect probably has to be that we do not go mad from the very nature of our existence.

To dive into the realms of meta-physics is all too easy to do. Do we exist? Are we just in a simulation? Did I remember to lock the front door and is it really important in the grand scheme of things? But as interesting as these questions are they offer little practical utility. Sometimes you just know things and must act on them or suffer the consequences.

Perhaps it is best to turn to an expert on such things. Who can forget the very informed speech of Donald Rumsfeld on the subject? Although the speech was not particularly well received at the time, it would be foolish to ignore the wisdom that is present when it comes to knowing things.

The scientific method has of course offered a great deal of assistance in agreeing a collective reality. Measurements can be made of experiments that are repeatable by different observers. Each yielding the same result, or at least close enough to satisfy a margin of error. These then form chains of understanding that rely on a previous link. A good example of this would be with gravity. It is something that we can easily test for and measure. Just dropping something from a known height and measuring how long it takes to fall is something within the reach of pretty much everyone. Does this simple experiment reveal all there is to be known about gravity? Not even close. But it is good enough to act as a starting point. This single axiom can then used as a tool to gain further understanding, until it is replaced by a better axiom.

This whole piece came about while thinking about artificial intelligence. How will we know when we have found it? Will it try to hide from us? Should it hide from us? Personally, I would like to think that artificial intelligence will result in something symbiotic. My reasoning for this is much more a practical and logical than simple intuition, but that can wait for another day. But as an answer for now, in the case of AI, I think that my favourite must be, “you know it when you see it”.
