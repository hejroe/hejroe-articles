# AI Paralysis
 
Disruptive technologies are, well, they are disruptive. Sometimes in a good way and sometimes in a bad way. At points, the benefits and changes are obvious, and at others, there is a great deal of uncertainty. We are, by most accounts, in the midst of an AI revolution, a period of unprecedented technological advancement that promises to reshape industries and redefine how we work and live. The headlines are relentless, filled with pronouncements of breakthroughs and statistics trumpeting widespread adoption.

But as I observe the commercial landscape, particularly within the small to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs or SMBs) that form the backbone of the UK economy, I sense something different. Beyond the surface-level engagement with new tools, there is more than just a hint of paralysis – a deep-seated hesitation and uncertainty as to how to handle, integrate, and genuinely utilise all these lovely new technologies being presented. Now, you would be well justified in stating that there is lots of press and figures shown talking about the uptake of AI. These are all very likely quite true, to varying degrees for some a bit perhaps. So just what am I getting at here? I believe there's a significant disconnect between the pervasive narrative of an AI gold rush and the tangible, transformative adoption within the everyday businesses that need clarity most.

## The Shifting Sands – When "Best Practice" Becomes Yesterday's News

To demonstrate this paralysis, I would ask you, if you were to start an AI business today to build a foundational model that could compete with a State of the Art (SotA) frontier model from one of the big names, how would you do it? Would you want to invest billions in creating massive data centres and infrastructure projects? Or perhaps embark on activities to harness huge swathes of data that can be used to train your latest AI offerings?

Mid to likely late 2024, you would be forgiven for shouting instantly, "we need to build data centres with thousands and thousands of GPUs/TPUs and collect/manufacture all the data we can!" But approaching mid-2025, the chorus for such an approach is a little more muted. After the revelations of smaller, highly efficient models like Deepseek and others, people started to question the wisdom of relying on a single, monolithic method. The ground beneath what constitutes "best practice" in AI development is not just shifting; it feels like it's in a constant state of liquefaction.

The cracks in the infrastructure/data-only theory that seems to have become dominant have been present all along. There are many notable names that have tried to state that the AI issue is not as solvable as some would suggest by relying on a limited set of approaches. Indeed, there is not a single example I can immediately think of from the natural world where the consumption of only huge amounts of data, both real and simulated, led to intelligent systems or even life itself. There seems to be more than data alone. For an SME owner, observing this high-level architectural turbulence, the idea of committing significant capital or resources to any single AI strategy must feel akin to building a house on quicksand.

## The "No-Brainer" AI vs. The Current Conundrum

Now, the field of AI is simply vast nowadays, in both theory and application. It's important to acknowledge that many of its implementations have been adopted as "no brainers," and rightly so. Security using AI detection tools, for instance, is a "no brainer"; the opportunities for a better service at lower cost are simply too compelling. I think it could also be said for other quite different industries. The use of AI in trading networks, for those who find such things compelling, again offers utility that makes it another "no brainer." Sensors in a car linked to some vision task – I am sure you can see the logic and rationale here. These are often targeted solutions to well-defined problems.

As impacting as these implementations can be in single markets and narrow verticals, none of them stand to influence business in quite the way that the current wave of generative AI and advanced robotics is developing. Currently, we are experiencing an amazing boost of innovation and improvement. Barely a week goes by without some incredible new model or tool being released that significantly improves or alters the AI domain, most noticeably in the areas of robotics and LLMs. The LLM technologies, in particular, are still advancing fast. Additional functionality is being integrated to give a better user experience with a more seamless workflow where text, images, and sound are all handled from a single prompt. The utility for LLMs is also considerable, with humanity eager to use such tools. Yet, this very richness and speed contribute to the problem.

## The User's Lament – "This is Not What I Want!"

For many SME owners and managers I speak to, or whose sentiments I gauge, the reaction to this deluge of ever-more-complex tools is a silent, frustrated scream of: "This is not what I want!" Businesses, especially smaller ones, are primarily concerned with their core function – be it crafting artisan goods, providing essential local services, or managing a retail space. They do not want to get embroiled in complex technological solutions requiring armies of consultants, lots of ongoing maintenance, and spiralling, unpredictable costs. They desire an actual, understandable solution to a real problem they have.

Think of it like the evolution of office software. For decades, to convey information, we've been pushed to become proficient in word processors, spreadsheet programs, presentation software, and now a myriad of cloud-sharing and collaboration platforms. Each is a separate application, requiring its own expertise. But the user's fundamental goal – to communicate, to analyse, to persuade – often gets lost in the struggle to master the tools. Why must one become an expert in things that do not relate to the activities one is actually interested in? Many SMEs look at the current AI landscape and see a similar, if not more daunting, fragmentation, demanding they become AI experts rather than simply better at their actual business. They're not necessarily looking for a hundred new AI tools; they're looking for integrated, intuitive support for their core commercial objectives.

## The Vendor-Reality Gap – Words vs. Actions

There feels to be some sort of gap between what large SotA AI vendors are saying and what is actually happening on the ground, especially at an SMB/SME level. First, it is probably useful to acknowledge some key features of the AI marketplace as a whole.

Big vendors are still hiring developers at a considerable rate. Yet some of their CEOs are simultaneously being quite vocal about how their AI solutions will significantly reduce or even remove the need for developers. They also talk, quite naturally, about how everyone is using their products and how powerful they are. But concrete, relatable evidence of just exactly where and how everyday businesses are deeply and transformatively using this technology seems less obvious, often drowned out by impressive but less relevant large enterprise case studies or consumer-focused metrics.

What do all the companies that have invested in implementing AI actually have? Is it something truly innovative and unique, with appropriate barriers to entry, deeply embedded into their core operations? Or is it, for many, simply an API call to a third-party service, a subscription to a new SaaS tool, and soon to be a "Model Context Protocol" typed solution? It may even implement other wonderful new technologies such as Google's own Agent-to-Agent protocol. Maybe it is something just as modest as a subscription or two for key users in specific roles. The interesting bit is that, for many, it is not the monumental, systemic shift in operations that has been spoken of. Again, this kind of talk, this mismatch between grand pronouncements and observable reality, increases the sense of uncertainty and fuels the paralysis.

## The Compounding Anxieties – Costs, Risks, and Broken Promises

The market perceptions for SMEs are not being helped by some of the economic signals emanating from the AI sector. Consider the reported discussions of OpenAI apparently discussing offering what amounts to a doctorate-level AI researcher for 20,000 (USD) per month. Such figures are simply astronomical for most SMEs. Meanwhile, smaller outfits are presenting SotA models that seem to offer similar or comparable performance for free, or at vastly lower costs. This creates immense confusion about value, cost, and what is truly necessary.

Then there is the issue of indemnity. It is not offered – well, not really. There seems to be some assurances in regard to protection from copyright claims if you use the "enterprise" versions of certain tools, but that really does seem to be the extent of it for most offerings. So just what are you buying, and what are your actual liabilities? For an SME, where legal budgets are tight and risks can be existential, this lack of clarity is a significant deterrent.

Add to this the sheer technological volatility. At any time, a SotA developer could release a new model, or even a subtle update, that effectively breaks all of your carefully crafted prompts, integrations, and systems, requiring costly rework. This isn't a hypothetical; it's a lived experience for many early adopters of rapidly evolving APIs. And if we look at the price points for more tangible AI, like robots, for those businesses where they might have utility, we encounter a significant range in options and prices. To add to the base costs, you also have to consider a host of other potential costs. Just the training aspects alone are likely to be at least noticeable, for both staff and customers. Will your robot manufacturer still be around in a year or two, or will they be owned by someone else with entirely different priorities? Will your expensive robots even be relevant in a couple of years' time, or will they be outdated curiosities? With the pace of change as it is, you would be forgiven for being exceedingly cautious.

The "human element" within SMEs also weighs heavily. The prospect of significant upskilling for a small team, the genuine fear of making valued colleagues' roles redundant, or the sheer managerial effort of change management in a lean organisation – these are not trivial concerns. They are core to the cautious approach many are taking. Furthermore, many SMEs look at their existing data – customer lists in spreadsheets, historical sales figures, operational notes – and struggle to see how this often imperfect, "small" data translates to the grand promises of AI, leading to a feeling of "data paralysis" before they even begin.

## The Wider Storm – External Pressures and the Allure of Inaction

Then there is a host of other, broader considerations that are impacting on the decision-making ability of organisations. Global tariffs, fluctuating taxes, wage pressures, evolving legislation (particularly around AI itself), geopolitical instability, wars – you know, the usual sort of stuff that makes long-term strategic planning a challenging endeavour.

All of this makes it very difficult to confidently move forward with significant new technological investments in a lot of cases. So what do people do at such times? Well, normally, just waiting to see where the crowd goes is not a completely terrible strategy, at least historically speaking. If everyone else is diving in and demonstrably succeeding with a particular approach, the risk for your own business feels lower. But with AI, the "crowd" itself seems hesitant and confused, at least at the SME level.

Earlier this year, we have seen massive shifts in the perception of value for a number of high-tech companies. Safe bets suddenly did not look so safe. Market leaders looked to be lagging. Self-proclaimed tech luminaries were not coming up with the goods that they had been alluding to. Despite the cocksure nature of some organisations, at least on the public face, there was, and still is, a great deal of uncertainty.

Now, just quite where that uncertainty comes from is another thing. The larger developers and vendors have a business model that works for them: large datacentres, lots of cloud and virtual services. But nation-states have other ideas, mulling over digital sovereignty, data localisation, and national security implications. Then, people also hold views and opinions – ethical concerns, fears of job displacement, distrust of "black box" algorithms – that can sink any mighty industrial titan if not addressed. Where it becomes more contentious is where these competing needs meet. Again, this only fertilises the speculative ground further. As a guiding principle, when what an entity says is different from what they do, then it creates uncertainty. An increase in uncertainty increases the effort and risks associated with actions taken. So the best action often becomes, or at least feels like, to just do the same thing as you are doing without increasing current risk.

## The Unsettling Horizon – Economic Realignments and Lingering Questions

Then there is, of course, the fact that the current economic model will likely need to adjust significantly in the face of mature AI and automation. With robots making everything for almost nothing (a theoretical endpoint, perhaps, but one that casts a long shadow) and technology advancing to a level where many immediate necessities are met with minimal human labour, then just what will the world be like? This is not to say that this is an immediate concern for the average SME deciding on a software upgrade. It is more to point out the background radiation of profound, systemic uncertainty. If you sell burgers and you only have robotic staff, then who will your customers be? The robotic staff from the garage or supermarket? With this, I hope to convey more the sense of a business, particularly an SME, being happy to wait a little while longer than normal to make big bets, as market growth or even market stability is difficult to rely upon under times of such profound uncertainty and stress. The very fabric of commerce is being questioned, and this, understandably, contributes to a wait-and-see approach.

## A Glimmer of Guidance?

This constellation of rapidly shifting technologies, vendor hype versus on-the-ground reality, economic anxieties, operational risks, and existential questions creates the "AI Paralysis" I observe. It's a rational response to a deeply irrational, or at least highly unpredictable, environment, especially for SMEs who lack the vast resources of global corporations to experiment and absorb failures. They are caught between the fear of missing out and the terror of a misstep.

I would suggest to all of this that there is actually a way forward for pretty much every organisation of any size or scale. However, finding such voices, ones that offer clear, pragmatic, and trustworthy guidance tailored to the realities of an SME in the current UK climate, seems a bit harder for now. The immediate task, perhaps, is simply to acknowledge the paralysis, understand its roots, and begin a more honest conversation about what businesses truly need to navigate this turbulent new era.
