# Pairs and More

Ever wonder why certain things seem to come in pairs? Eyes, ears, limbs and a host of others. There are even times where the World demands more than just pairs. In fact, it would seem that redundancy, planned or not, is present in many places.

There are of course other advantages to having pairs of things. Both eyes and ears are obvious first choices as they allow for the World to have three dimensions. This allows for us to be able to use the information present in our field of experience to be better utilised. If you doubt the power of this, try closing one eye and then making a grab for something moving, just as if an animal of some sort attempting to catch moving prey. Even with practice you can expect to be less performant than if you had access to the use of both eyes.

With two sources of information it is possible to make far better predictions about the environment. At least in the case of movement. Sound also benefits from different points of observation, as it allows for us to locate the direction of a noise. Then there is the instance of two nostrils... . This is where perhaps this analogy breaks down a bit, but I stand by my point with the eyes and ears.

Nature often gives us examples that we can learn a great deal from. Rarely, if ever, does there seem to be a single solution to the problem of life. How many legs, arms, heads and all the other paraphernalia that comes with living things is not a fixed number.

## Costs of Robustness

Having more of one thing also gives us contingency should one of a thing fail or be damaged beyond repair, for whatever reason. Losing an eye does not mean we would be completely helpless. Even if losing both eyes we have other senses to fall back on. The same can be said for other parts of us that have been considered critical systems for one reason or another. Arms, legs, lungs, kidneys, and others I am sure.

There are many parts of us which there is only one of. The heart, stomach, liver, brain and more. The reasons for this likely stem from the expense of such things. The energy cost of having two brains for instance is likely to outweigh any immediate benefits and therefore evolution has not deemed it necessary to pursue this path. But there are many creatures that do have more than one brain, as with the octopus. Even thinking of a brain as a whole distributed nervous system also creates some questions over what exactly a brain is are where its centre is. There are some that also argue our own brains have complexities in structure that allude to hierarchical systems or even competing functions that do equate to separate brains, but where they have been clumped into one locality.

Irrespective there is some balance to be had between the systems we have to collect and process information and the effort required to support such systems. We have significant evidence in us, and from other life on our planet that shows different paths are being explored by evolutionary processes.

## More is Better

It is hard to argue against having more of a thing is normally better, at least in many circumstances. More space, more time, more resources, and a host of others are considered desirable. This is evident in our senses. We have more than one sense and more than one biological mechanism for detecting information/data from the World we live in. The reason for this is that in the case of senses and our ability to perceive the World, more seems better. By taking in the sights, sounds, smells (to name a few) we are able to better understand and predict how the World unfolds. By being able to make better predictions we can better utilise scant resources to acquire new resources with the greater efficiency.

When considering an average person, they have a number of senses all working in conjunction with each other. They form a network of cooperative understanding. If one of the senses starts to report information that seems counter intuitive to the data reported from the other senses, then we are likely to notice the oddness. Your food could look, feel and smell perfectly fine but still taste awful. At which point you would likely look to get rid of said unpleasantness and avoid illness.

## Evolution Has Not Stopped

The senses we possess have not stopped evolving. Every new generation is still prone to subtle differences that can alter our physical capacity to sense new forms of data, for instance in the case of Tetrachromacy, where 4 colour receptors are present in eyes as opposed to the more normal number of 3 in humans. It is also just as possible that senses will be morphed into obsolescence as humanity and our environments change.

With the ingenuity we humans have, there are new ways to sense the World around by our own design. All manner of sensors at different scales that give us new information with which to make sense of the Universe we live in. More importantly this information allows us to better predict how the World will be, and to use that knowledge to our advantage. There are many augmentations we have that allow us to collect information far beyond those we were endowed with at birth.

There is no prospect of evolution even ending. Can the Universe produce "the perfect form"? Personally I suspect not, despite having watched Alien. One of the reasons for this is due to evolution seemingly liking certain "designs" but never being able to settle on it. This is described in the case of carcinisation and decarcinisation, where sea-based crustaceans become more or less crab like. It is a process that has constant movement and change, which may even be intrinsic to the process itself.

Already humanity has altered its evolutionary path. Our sciences throughout the ages have massively altered our ability to overcome the adverse events that would previously limit lifespans. Then there is the prospect of humanity being able to alter even further the physical attributes we have. This will also be extended to augmentations, as is already the case.

## How Much Is Enough?

Is there a limit to the amount of sensory information we can have? Will too much simply overload us to the point of inaction or harm? Or will the mechanisms that we have already be so elastic that they simply cater to the additional information, and it is parsed just as smaller information sets would be?

Equally we need to consider the amount of precision required in order for it to offer some utility. In other words, how can we avoid over measuring? What do we miss if we under measure? These are tricky questions to answer and are further complicated by other interesting facets of the problem environment. For instance, our sense of taste would appear to operate at a quantum level and not just at atomic or greater levels as evidenced by the paper "Sweet taste of heavy water". To me this is quite the revelation, to have such a vast spectrum of scale in input data is simply astounding.

Already we use external systems to enhance our senses. With Augmented Reality we can perform all manner of additional observations that elude our natural abilities. We can see heat, or noise. If desired, we can turn colours into sounds. These technologies are still very much in their infancy and there is still much to be discovered.
